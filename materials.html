Stone that suits how you actually cook

We buy from the major slab yards in Dallas and Fort Worth, which means you're choosing from thousands of real slabs — not a rack of two-inch samples. Here's how the materials differ where it matters.

Granite

Quarried natural stone. Every slab is unique, it shrugs off a hot skillet straight from the burner, and it's harder to scratch than almost anything else in your kitchen. Seal it about once a year — a ten-minute job we'll show you how to do.

Best for: heavy cooks, busy families, anyone who wants a countertop with no two spots the same.

Quartz

Ground natural quartz bound with resin, so it's non-porous: no sealing, ever, and wine or turmeric won't stain it. The pattern is engineered, so what you see on the sample is what lands in your kitchen. Keep a trivet handy — resin scorches around 300°F.

Best for: white kitchens, marble looks without the upkeep, and anyone who wants zero maintenance.

Quartzite

Natural sandstone metamorphosed into something harder than granite. It gives you the soft, veiny marble look in a stone that can actually take kitchen abuse. It costs more and it's slower to fabricate, and it's worth both.

Best for: the marble look on an island you'll actually use.

Marble & soapstone

Marble is the real thing — beautiful, soft, and it will etch where lemon juice sits. Soapstone is dense, heat-proof and darkens with mineral oil. Both develop a patina, which is either the whole point or a dealbreaker; we'll be honest with you about which.

Best for: baking counters, bar areas, powder baths, and people who like stone that ages.

Care

Living with your stone

None of this is complicated, and we leave a printed version of it on your counter when we go.

  • Daily: warm water and a little dish soap. Skip the vinegar, bleach and anything labeled "for granite" at the grocery store — most of it builds up a film.
  • Spills: blot, don't wipe, especially oil and red wine on natural stone. Sealed granite gives you plenty of time.
  • Heat: granite and quartzite take a hot pan. Quartz doesn't — keep a trivet within reach of the cooktop.
  • Cutting: use a board. Not because the stone will scratch, but because your knives will lose.
  • Sealing: splash water on the counter once a year. If it beads, you're fine. If it darkens the stone, it's time to reseal.
